- If a country has **5 sources**, and each source improves by **+4 points**, the CPI improves by **+4** (because it’s an average).

## 3) Corruption ↔ NPA: a theoretical linkage model (cold, mechanical)

### 3A) The causal chain (simple DAG)
Think of GNPA as the output of **credit quality + recognition + recovery**:

**(1) Corruption / capture in credit decisions → (2) lower underwriting quality → (3) higher default risk → (4) higher NPA formation**  
AND  
**(1) Corruption / capture in enforcement & courts → (5) slower / weaker recovery → (6) NPAs stay longer & bigger**  

So corruption affects both:
- **Inflow to NPAs (slippages)**  
- **Outflow from NPAs (recoveries / resolutions)**  

---

### 3B) A compact mathematical model
Let:

- \(L_t\) = total loans  
- \(N_t\) = stock of NPAs  
- \(s_t\) = slippage rate (new NPAs created)  
- \(r_t\) = recovery/resolution rate (NPAs resolved/recovered)  
- \(g_t\) = loan growth rate  

Then:

\[
N_{t+1} = N_t + (s_t \cdot L_t) - (r_t \cdot N_t)
\]

GNPA ratio:

\[
\text{GNPA}_{t} = \frac{N_t}{L_t}
\]

Now introduce a **governance/corruption factor** \(C_t\) (higher = worse):

- Underwriting gets weaker when corruption rises:
\[
s_t = s_0 + \alpha C_t + \beta(\text{macro stress})
\]
- Recovery gets weaker when corruption rises:
\[
r_t = r_0 - \gamma C_t + \delta(\text{resolution strength like IBC})
\]

So the model says:
- Higher \(C_t\) raises slippages \(s_t\)
- Higher \(C_t\) reduces recoveries \(r_t\)
- Strong resolution frameworks (IBC) raise \(r_t\) and also reduce future \(s_t\) by disciplining borrowers/promoters

That’s exactly why a system can see GNPA fall sharply after reforms even if “corruption perception” doesn’t jump quickly: the banking system can improve its recovery machine faster than national integrity perceptions move.

## 3) CPI methodology (what it measures, what it doesn’t)
**What CPI is:** a **composite perception index** of **public-sector corruption** based on **expert assessments + business surveys** (not “common people bribery count”). Each country needs **≥3 sources** (out of up to 13). citeturn12search3turn12search6turn12search2

### The math (simplified but accurate)
Let a country have \(k\) usable sources this year (k ≥ 3).  
Each source is **rescaled to 0–100** (after standardization to keep comparability across years). Then:

\[
\text{CPI} = \text{round}\left(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k} s_i\right)
\]

Where each \(s_i\) is the rescaled score from source \(i\). citeturn12search6turn12search3

**So a country improves CPI by improving the underlying signals that those expert/business sources measure**: prosecution credibility, procurement fairness, judicial independence, political finance transparency, administrative discretion, etc.

# 4️⃣ How Is the Score Calculated?

Each source has its own scale (e.g., 1–7, 0–4, etc.).

Step-by-step:

1️⃣ Convert each source to a 0–100 scale  
2️⃣ Standardize to maintain comparability across years  
3️⃣ Average all available sources  
4️⃣ Round final result  
5️⃣ Publish confidence interval  

Mathematically simplified:

CPI = mean (standardized source scores)

If India has 8 usable sources:

CPI = (S1 + S2 + S3 + S4 + S5 + S6 + S7 + S8) / 8

Where each Si is 0–100 standardized.

It is imperfect.

- Private sector fraud
- Household bribery directly
- Petty vs grand corruption separately
- Exact monetary scale
- Improvements that are very recent

It is slow-moving.

## 3) CPI vs “actual convictions” — why they can mismatch

- enforcement may be selective,

**So a “conviction count” series and a “CPI score” series are measuring different things**, and can move differently without contradiction.
