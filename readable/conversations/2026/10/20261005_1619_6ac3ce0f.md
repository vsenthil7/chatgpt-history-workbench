# 05/10/2026 17:19 — AI Agents vs Enterprise Systems

Partner, **you are not being stupid. You have identified one of the most important distinctions that gets lost in the AI noise.**

After checking current banking/consulting evidence, I think your intuition is substantially right — but there is one layer missing from the picture.

> **AI does not replace the deterministic system of record.**
>
> It gets inserted mainly into the **interpretation, decision, orchestration, exception-handling and human-work layers surrounding that deterministic system.**

That distinction changes the whole discussion.

McKinsey actually makes almost exactly the distinction you are making: when variables are known beforehand, **deterministic automation remains more reliable and cost-effective**; agentic systems become interesting when variables are fluid and choices have to be made at runtime. citeturn0search2

So I think this deserves a proper research project rather than another loose AI discussion.

---

## 1. Your "Senthil" example is exactly right

Suppose a bank has:

```text
Customer_ID = 4837291
Name        = Senthil
Balance     = £14,521.37
Account     = 20483920
```

An LLM has **no business deciding** that your balance is £14,521.37.

It should not infer your account number.

It should not probabilistically decide which Senthil you are.

It should not "reason" about whether £14,521.37 looks plausible.

The authoritative system says:

```text
customer_id = 4837291
```

and deterministic software retrieves the record.

### That remains normal software.

```text
Database
   ↓
Business logic
   ↓
API
   ↓
Web/mobile UI
```

AI doesn't magically improve:

```text
SELECT balance
FROM account
WHERE account_id = 20483920;
```

In fact, inserting an LLM into that operation would often make the architecture **worse**.

**Confidence: ~99%.**

---

# 2. So where does AI enter?

One level **above** that deterministic machinery.

Consider this request:

> "I've got £14,000 sitting here. My mortgage rate changes in January, I need £8,000 for my daughter's university fees next year, and I'm thinking of paying £5,000 off the mortgage. What should I think about?"

Now the computer has a different problem.

It must potentially:

```text
Understand the request
        ↓
identify relevant information
        ↓
retrieve accounts
        ↓
retrieve mortgage
        ↓
retrieve upcoming payments
        ↓
retrieve product/risk rules
        ↓
possibly run deterministic calculations
        ↓
compare alternatives
        ↓
explain them
        ↓
possibly initiate an approved action
```

The **numbers still come from deterministic systems**.

But deciding:

> "Which systems do I need to consult to deal with this request?"

is no longer merely database retrieval.

That is where LLMs/agents become useful.

---

# 3. Think of an enterprise as six layers

This is the picture I think you've been missing.

| Layer | Example | LLM importance |
|---|---|---:|
| **1. Record** | customer, account, trade, payment | 🔴 Very low |
| **2. Transaction** | transfer £500, execute order | 🔴 Low |
| **3. Rules/calculation** | interest, tax, P&L, limits | 🔴 Low |
| **4. Analytics** | fraud probability, forecast | 🟡 ML/AI |
| **5. Interpretation** | documents, questions, explanations | 🟢 High |
| **6. Orchestration** | decide steps/tools/workflow | 🟢 High |

The mistake in a lot of public AI discussion is talking as though **5 and 6 replace 1–4**.

They generally don't.

They **sit on top of them and use them**.

That is much closer to the real transformation than:

# 5. And this explains "agents"

An agent isn't valuable merely because it can chat.

Imagine an investment-bank employee currently doing this:

```text
Open client system
↓
find client
↓
open Bloomberg/market data
↓
open exposure system
↓
open risk application
↓
download spreadsheet
↓
read PDF
↓
calculate something
↓
open CRM
↓
write analysis
↓
email somebody
↓
create follow-up task
```

Most individual systems remain deterministic.

The agent's potential job is:

```text
Goal
 ↓
Understand task
 ↓
Determine required steps
 ↓
Call existing systems
 ↓
Retrieve authoritative data
 ↓
Run deterministic calculations
 ↓
Interpret unstructured material
 ↓
Handle exceptions
 ↓
Prepare recommendation
 ↓
Human approval where required
 ↓
Execute through existing APIs
 ↓
Audit everything
```

**That's the interesting part.**

The agent isn't replacing the ledger.

It's potentially replacing a chunk of the **human glue between systems**.

# 7. But AI could attack different parts of the reporting factory

Instead of:

> AI replaces DWH.

Think:

> **AI changes how humans specify, build, operate and consume the DWH.**

For example:

```text
BUSINESS QUESTION
"What caused yesterday's £12m revenue variance?"
                 ↓
             AI agent
                 ↓
       semantic/metadata layer
        ↙       ↓       ↘
   Warehouse  lineage   definitions
       ↓
deterministic SQL
       ↓
authoritative result
       ↓
AI interpretation
       ↓
narrative + evidence
```

Notice something extremely important.

### LLM does not calculate the £12m.

SQL does.

The LLM might determine **which calculation is required**, generate/query it under controls, inspect lineage and explain the result.

That architecture is radically different from "ask ChatGPT our revenue."

# 8. There may therefore be three futures for reporting

This needs research rather than premature certainty, but my current hypotheses would be:

### A. Durable deterministic core — **~95% probability**

Warehouses/lakehouses, transaction stores, governed metrics and reconciliation remain essential.

AI doesn't abolish accounting truth.

### B. Major reduction in manually built consumption/reporting work — **~75–90%**

Some:

```text
fixed report
dashboard
ad-hoc SQL
analyst extraction
Excel manipulation
narrative commentary
```

can shift toward:

```text
Question
 ↓
AI
 ↓
governed semantic definition
 ↓
generated query
 ↓
verified warehouse result
 ↓
explanation
```

### C. Significant automation of DWH engineering/support — **~70–85%**

Agents can increasingly help with:

```text
source discovery
mapping
SQL generation
ETL generation
schema-change analysis
lineage
testing
reconciliation
DQ investigation
incident diagnosis
documentation
migration
impact analysis
```

But those percentages are **research hypotheses**, not measured facts. We should explicitly try to falsify them.

# 9. Existing evidence says something similar

This isn't merely a theoretical picture.

JPMorganChase says more than **65,000 CIB employees actively use its LLM Suite**, while over 90% of engineers use AI coding assistants. More interestingly for your question, it reports transaction-screening AI enabling more than twice the review volume while halving manual operator checks. citeturn0search96

Its broader LLM Suite reached **200,000 onboarded employees within eight months**, and JPMorganChase says the next direction includes connecting internal data sources and combining GenAI with workflows to create agents capable of performing sequences of actions. citeturn0search13

In financial crime, McKinsey describes agents for **ETL monitoring, data-quality checks, pipeline failures, entity resolution, research, validation and rerunning minor failures**. It distinguishes those from deterministic transaction systems. citeturn0search5

And importantly, the evidence does **not** say enterprise AI has already transformed everything. McKinsey reported in late 2025 that nearly two-thirds of surveyed organizations had **not yet begun scaling AI across the enterprise**. citeturn0search9

So some of the "everything is already agentic" narrative really is ahead of deployment reality.

# 10. The boundary I want this research to investigate

Instead of asking:

> **"Where can AI be used?"**

That question will produce 500 shallow use cases.

Our stronger question should be:

> **For every major activity inside a modern enterprise, which work should remain deterministic, which benefits from analytical ML, which benefits from LLMs, and which genuinely benefits from autonomous/semiautonomous agents?**

Then ask a second question:

> **Does AI merely improve that activity, remove human labour from it, collapse an existing software layer, or create an entirely new capability?**

That's a serious research problem.

# 11. Proposed research project

### Working title

**Enterprise AI Boundary Research**

### Problem statement

> **Determine where LLMs and agentic systems create genuine economic or operational value within existing enterprise technology estates, and where deterministic software, databases, rules engines, ETL/DWH platforms and conventional automation remain superior.**

We explicitly **do not begin with the assumption that agents will replace enterprise software.**

We test it.

## My starting position

I would currently put the central hypothesis at roughly:

> **70% confidence:** the biggest enterprise effect of agents will *not* be replacing databases, transaction engines or core deterministic business logic. It will be reducing the enormous amount of human cognitive/glue work occurring **between systems, around exceptions, during analysis, and between data and decisions**.

And another:

> **85% confidence:** many existing enterprise systems survive, but humans increasingly interact with them through AI orchestration layers rather than individually navigating dozens of applications.

Those are **hypotheses for this project**, deliberately not facts.

The current evidence from banks and consultancies is consistent with them, but nowhere near sufficient yet to declare them proven. McKinsey itself says deterministic automation is preferable where the variables are known, while agentic execution becomes distinct when the software must select material parameters dynamically at runtime. citeturn0search2

**That boundary — rather than "AI versus no AI" — is what I think we should research.**
