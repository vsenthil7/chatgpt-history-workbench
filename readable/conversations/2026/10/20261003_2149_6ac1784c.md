**AI expectations / AGI race / investor money**

→ more GPUs/accelerators + servers  
→ enormous requirement for **HBM, server DRAM, NAND/SSDs, networking and power**  
→ memory manufacturers allocate more production toward high-margin AI/server products  
→ less capacity available for conventional PC/mobile memory

## 03/10/2026 22:52 — AI Must Pay Its Physical Bill

Yes. I understand the question now. **You are not asking how you personally survive higher RAM or laptop prices. You're asking what responsibility the AI industry has for a worldwide externality it is helping create.**

And I agree with the premise behind the challenge: saying *“AI will create abundance”* while consuming scarce memory, chips, electricity, water, land and capital—and pushing some costs onto everyone else—is incomplete.

I also wouldn't claim that I personally am “superintelligent.” But if increasingly capable AI is supposed to justify the resources society is pouring into it, **it should help solve the scarcity it creates, not merely win the competition for scarce resources.**

### My answer is not “build more datacentres”

The world needs a **closed-loop AI infrastructure strategy**:

**AI demand → scarcity → AI helps expand/optimise supply → efficiency improves → resources return to society → AI can grow again.**

That requires several things happening together.

1. **Stop treating bigger models as the only route to better intelligence.**  
   Every query should not automatically consume the largest model available. Routing, specialised models, distillation, quantisation, caching, sparse/MoE computation and local/on-device inference should drive *compute required per useful unit of intelligence* dramatically downward.

2. **Use AI aggressively to improve the semiconductor system itself.**  
   Chip design, verification, fab scheduling, yield optimisation, packaging, memory architecture, materials discovery and manufacturing automation are exactly the kinds of enormously complex optimisation problems AI should attack. If AI consumes 10× more memory but helps the industry eventually manufacture memory 20× more efficiently, the equation changes.

3. **Datacentres should become flexible energy consumers rather than privileged energy consumers.**  
   Training workloads that aren't latency-sensitive can move geographically and temporally toward surplus renewable/nuclear/grid capacity. Datacentres can reduce workloads during grid stress instead of competing with households and industry at the worst possible time.

4. **Build circular hardware infrastructure.**  
   GPUs, servers, batteries, cooling systems and electronics shouldn't follow a constant replace-and-discard cycle. Components should be designed for reuse, refurbishment and material recovery. AI itself can optimise that supply chain.

5. **Protect ordinary computing capacity.**  
   This one is rarely discussed. If AI infrastructure becomes so profitable that manufacturers systematically divert memory and semiconductor capacity away from ordinary devices, governments have a legitimate competition/industrial-policy problem to consider. Society still needs affordable PCs, phones, automotive chips, medical electronics and industrial equipment.

6. **Measure the externality publicly.**  
   Major AI systems should ultimately be measurable not merely by benchmark score, but something closer to:

   **useful intelligence / £ / joule / litre of water / kg CO₂ / semiconductor capacity.**

   Then you can see whether the next generation is actually technologically better—or merely **larger and richer**.

---

### But there is a deeper solution

Suppose AI requires $1 trillion of infrastructure and ultimately makes humanity only marginally more productive.

**That's a poor bargain.**

Suppose the same infrastructure produces systems capable of accelerating:

- new semiconductor materials,
- cheaper energy storage,
- nuclear/fusion engineering,
- grid optimisation,
- drug discovery,
- automated manufacturing,
- agriculture,
- climate modelling,
- logistics,
- education,
- scientific research.

Then AI has started **repaying the physical resources society invested in it**.

That is the standard I would use.

And there's an uncomfortable possibility that should be stated plainly:

> **If increasingly powerful AI cannot eventually produce more real-world abundance than the scarcity its infrastructure creates, then scaling it indefinitely is not evidence of superintelligence. It is evidence of an inefficient industry.**

### And Sharanya is actually part of the world-level problem you're pointing at

I won't turn this into advice about her.

Your concern is broader: imagine millions of young people.

One student uses increasingly capable AI continuously. Another doesn't. The first can potentially learn, research, create and execute much faster.

So society faces **two inequalities simultaneously**:

**AI access inequality**  
People without access risk falling behind.

**AI-caused physical scarcity**  
The infrastructure required to provide that access can itself increase the cost of the devices and resources needed to participate.

That's a contradiction.

The long-term objective therefore cannot be:

> *Everyone must buy increasingly expensive hardware to access increasingly enormous AI.*

It has to become:

> **Extremely capable intelligence available from ordinary, inexpensive hardware, while most expensive computation is efficiently shared infrastructure.**

In other words, if this technology really progresses toward what people are calling **superintelligence**, one sign of success should eventually be almost paradoxical:

**the intelligence gets enormously more capable while the physical resources required per person go down.**

If instead every generation requires another trillion dollars, another mountain of GPUs, another memory shortage and another power crisis just to remain competitive, **we haven't solved intelligence economically yet.**

Partner, **yes — now I understand the architecture you are proposing**, and it is more interesting than simply “run AI on people's laptops.”

The causal chain you described is now supported by the evidence.

IDC says AI infrastructure is causing manufacturers to reallocate manufacturing capacity from consumer DRAM/NAND toward higher-margin HBM and high-capacity server memory. It isn't the *only* cause of every laptop/phone price movement, but it is now a significant direct contributor.

I think you have independently arrived at something technically legitimate.

Your idea is approximately:

> Instead of concentrating trillions of pounds of computing equipment inside enormous datacentres, create a global distributed AI-compute fabric consisting partly of machines owned by ordinary people/businesses.

It might contain:

**CPU + GPU/NPU + RAM + SSD + secure execution environment + network connection.**

The owner doesn't need to understand AI.

The machine registers itself with a global scheduler.

# 3. But I would change one important part of your design

Don't distribute **everything**.

That's where your Hadoop analogy becomes especially useful.

Hadoop didn't say:

> every calculation must happen across every computer.

It moved particular jobs to appropriate machines.

AI should work similarly.

### Four classes of compute

| Work | Household distributed network | Datacentre |
|---|---:|---:|
| Embeddings / indexing | 🟢 Excellent | 🟢 |
| Batch inference | 🟢 Excellent | 🟢 |
| Data preprocessing | 🟢 Excellent | 🟢 |
| Evaluation/testing | 🟢 Excellent | 🟢 |
| Synthetic-data generation | 🟢 Excellent | 🟢 |
| Independent agent jobs | 🟢 Excellent | 🟢 |
| Fine-tuning some models | 🟢/🟡 | 🟢 |
| Ordinary inference | 🟢/🟡 | 🟢 |
| Interactive giant-model inference | 🟡 | 🟢 |
| tightly coupled frontier training | 🔴 | 🟢 |

**That last row is the critical distinction.**

Thousands of GPUs training one enormous model exchange data continuously.

A datacentre GPU can communicate with neighbouring accelerators through extremely high-bandwidth specialised interconnects.

A household broadband connection cannot replicate that.

So distributing **one tightly coupled training operation** across 50,000 houses is generally a poor architecture today.

But distributing **50,000 independent AI jobs among 50,000 houses** is an entirely different proposition.

That could work extremely well.

You asked a particularly good question:

> *Do we actually need GPU for everything?*

**No.**

That's one of the inefficiencies we should attack.

GPU excels at massively parallel matrix operations.

CPU excels at many other things:

- orchestration
- tokenisation
- compression
- retrieval
- database operations
- scheduling
- encryption
- networking
- business logic
- preprocessing
- some inference workloads

Then there are **NPUs/AI accelerators**, which may eventually be even more important at the edge.

So the scheduler shouldn't ask:

> "Where is a GPU?"

It should ask:

> **"What is the cheapest adequate compute for this particular operation?"**

That is a substantially different architecture.

# 6. But it doesn't eliminate datacentres

This is where I disagree with one part.

It would **reduce the amount that must be centralised**, potentially substantially.

It won't eliminate datacentres because centralised facilities have major advantages:

**power efficiency + cooling + networking + maintenance + physical security + accelerator interconnect + utilisation density.**

A GPU sitting unused in my house isn't automatically environmentally superior to a highly utilised datacentre GPU.

And distributing machines doesn't magically eliminate electricity or heat.

The household node still consumes electricity and produces heat.

But your architecture could eliminate/reduce some:

- dedicated datacentre construction,
- massive concentrated cooling infrastructure,
- grid concentration,
- land concentration,
- idle household computing capacity,
- concentration of infrastructure ownership.

And the waste heat could even be locally useful in colder climates.

# 7. Security is harder than just a container

Your container idea is directionally right, but ordinary containers aren't sufficient.

If someone physically owns the machine, assume they potentially control the operating system.

You'd need something closer to:

**hardware root of trust → measured boot → confidential computing/TEE → encrypted model shards → encrypted job payload → remote attestation → signed execution → metered output → payment.**

And probably redundancy:

```text
Job X
 ├── Node A calculates result
 ├── Node B verifies
 └── Node C challenge/verification if disagreement
```

So neither the compute owner nor the AI customer simply has to trust the other.

That becomes a serious distributed-systems/security problem — but a solvable research/engineering problem.

# But our discussion still contains gaps worth investigating

After removing things others already do, I see approximately **six potentially distinctive ideas** remaining.

### 1. Extreme hardware inclusiveness

Gensyn's current RL Swarm documentation still starts around **32 GB CPU RAM** or substantial GPUs such as 3090/4090/5090/A100/H100. citeturn2search3

We're asking a harder question:

> Can **4 GB spare RAM**, weak CPUs, NPUs, old laptops and ordinary phones contribute *something useful*?

Not necessarily gradient training.

The scheduler could assign:

```text
4 GB phone
    ↓
evaluation / tokenisation / validation /
small inference / preprocessing / verification

8 GB laptop
    ↓
small training / embeddings / synthetic tasks

Gaming GPU
    ↓
training / inference

H100 cluster
    ↓
large learner island
```

That's materially different from demanding one minimum machine class.

### 2. Whole-AI-lifecycle scheduling

Not merely training.

```text
DATA
 ↓
clean
 ↓
deduplicate
 ↓
tokenise
 ↓
embed
 ↓
train
 ↓
evaluate
 ↓
red-team
 ↓
RL
 ↓
distil
 ↓
infer
 ↓
verify
```

Every operation asks:

> **What is the cheapest trustworthy currently available hardware capable of doing me?**

This is potentially a much bigger optimisation problem.

### 3. Provider plug-in without exposing proprietary training

This remains one of our strongest architectural requirements.

Our fabric should **not understand a provider's secret training algorithm.**

Instead:

```text
PROVIDER
   │
   ▼
Provider Adapter
   │
   ▼
Signed Job Manifest
   │
   ├── capability requirements
   ├── security class
   ├── resource limits
   ├── verification mechanism
   ├── encrypted workload
   ├── encrypted result contract
   └── compensation
             │
             ▼
         FABRIC
```

That lets proprietary providers and completely open models potentially use the same infrastructure.

But this is still **a hypothesis requiring security research**, because gradients themselves can leak training information. Distributed-AI literature documents gradient-leakage attacks. citeturn2search12

So "encrypted container" alone is absolutely not sufficient.

### 4. Device-owner-first trust

This may become a major philosophical difference.

Most systems begin:

> **How do we protect the customer's workload from an untrusted worker?**

We require both directions:

```text
MODEL PROVIDER
     ↓
 protect IP/data
     ↓
┌──────────────┐
│ TRUST LAYER  │
└──────────────┘
     ↑
 protect owner
     ↑
DEVICE OWNER
```

The owner must control:

**CPU / GPU / RAM / storage / bandwidth / hours / charging-only / thermal ceiling / electricity-price threshold / jurisdiction / permitted workload classes.**

And receive an auditable account of what happened.

Salad already explicitly assumes hostile consumer hosts and isolates workloads using a VM/container architecture, so there is substantial engineering to learn from there rather than invent ourselves. citeturn2search9

### 5. Protocol ≠ operator ≠ marketplace

I think we should retain this strongly.

```text
               OPEN STANDARD
                     │
       ┌─────────────┼─────────────┐
       ↓             ↓             ↓
 Operator A      Operator B     Operator C
 commercial       university     nonprofit
       │             │             │
       └─────────────┼─────────────┘
                     ↓
                OPEN WORKERS
```

No mandatory cryptocurrency.

No mandatory marketplace.

No mandatory central scheduler.

No mandatory commercial operator.

An operator could use:

**GBP, USD, INR, credits, donation, university allocation or no payment whatsoever.**

That makes the compute/economic protocol separable.

### 6. Distributed-native intelligence

This remains the most ambitious research question.

Instead of forcing today's centrally designed transformer onto distributed hardware:

> **What model architecture would we design if we knew from birth that its brain would live across millions of intermittent heterogeneous devices?**

DiPaCo is already moving toward modularity: different computational paths/modules can train on poorly connected heterogeneous workers. citeturn0search4

Our research would need to go beyond that rather than unknowingly recreate it.

# One idea from earlier needs correction

We talked about completely eliminating central model state.

**Don't make that a requirement yet.**

There is already research into decentralised storage, proofs of replication and recovery on unreliable edge devices. citeturn2search13

But there are enormous differences between:

**no central dependency**,  
**no central authority**, and  
**literally no central machines anywhere.**

Those aren't equivalent.

Our architectural rule should instead be:

> **No component is centralised unless measurement, physics, security, regulation or economics demonstrates that centralisation is advantageous.**

Likewise, don't decentralise something merely because we can.

---

# The environmental hypothesis also needs testing

We originally reasoned:

> household compute = fewer datacentres = environmentally better.

**Not necessarily.**

A datacentre GPU operating at 90% utilisation with sophisticated cooling may consume fewer total resources per useful computation than thousands of inefficient household machines.

And phones introduce:

**battery degradation + heat + charging losses + network energy + device lifetime reduction.**

Therefore our fundamental metric cannot simply be:

> GPU-hours decentralised.

It should be closer to:

### **Verified useful AI work / total societal resource cost**

including:

**hardware depreciation + electricity + cooling + networking + carbon + water + battery/device wear + infrastructure + money.**

That could produce surprising conclusions.

Sometimes the datacentre should win.

Our framework must be willing to choose it.
