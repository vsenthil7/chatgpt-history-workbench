# 04/10/2026 09:15 — AI Growth vs Internet/Web Era

Partner, after looking back at **what people actually said during the Internet/web transition**, rather than viewing the 1990s through today's hindsight, I think there is a very important conclusion:

> **AI is repeating several patterns from the Internet revolution — but it is not merely another Internet-sized technology.**
>
> The Internet dramatically reduced the cost of **communication, publishing, distribution and transactions**.
>
> AI is dramatically reducing the cost of **cognition, software creation and execution of knowledge work**.
>
> And agentic AI begins reducing the cost of **coordinating and operating work itself**.

That difference matters enormously.

---

## 1. First: remember what the Internet actually looked like in 1995

Today we look backwards and think:

> “Obviously the Internet was going to change everything.”

That is **not how it looked while people were living through it.**

In October 1995, Pew described Internet usage as experiencing **“explosive growth”**, but found that few users considered online activities essential. Online purchasing was barely happening. citeturn0search5

Think about how reasonable the scepticism sounded:

- Why buy something online when shops already exist?
- Why read news on a computer?
- Why send electronic mail when phones/faxes/mail exist?
- Who is going to put their credit card number into a website?
- How do you know information on a website is true?
- What about viruses?
- What about privacy?
- Won't people become isolated?
- Who actually needs a website?
- How will Internet companies ever make money?

These weren't stupid objections.

**Many were correct.**

The mistake was assuming that because the technology had serious problems, it wouldn't transform society.

---

# 2. What people worried about then vs what we worry about now

There's an almost eerie correspondence.

| Internet era | AI era |
|---|---|
| “Information online may be false.” | “AI hallucinates.” |
| “Who can trust a website?” | “Who can trust an AI answer?” |
| “Credit cards online aren't safe.” | “Don't give AI confidential data.” |
| “Viruses/hackers will increase.” | “AI will increase cyberattacks.” |
| “Privacy will disappear.” | “Models will consume private/IP data.” |
| “People will spend too much time online.” | “People will depend too much on AI.” |
| “People will become less social.” | “People will replace human interaction with AI.” |
| “Computers will eliminate jobs.” | “AI will eliminate jobs.” |
| “Digital divide will increase inequality.” | “AI divide will increase inequality.” |
| “Anyone can publish rubbish.” | “Anyone can generate unlimited rubbish.” |
| “Websites will destroy newspapers/shops.” | “AI will destroy knowledge professions.” |
| “The Internet is overhyped.” | “AI is a bubble.” |

And these Internet fears weren't reconstructed afterwards.

For example, by 2000 more than half of Internet users worried about bad medical information or computer viruses, nearly half worried about false news or financial information, and only 10% were very confident their online activities were private. citeturn0search7

In 1999, **54% of Americans expressed some concern that computers and technology were invading privacy.** citeturn0search10

So today's AI debate feels new.

Historically, much of its structure isn't.

# 3. But look at the job argument — this is fascinating

Around the Internet/computer transition, Americans weren't overwhelmingly expecting technological unemployment.

Around 2000:

- **43%** thought computers would create more jobs.
- **32%** expected fewer jobs.
- roughly one quarter expected little difference. citeturn1search1

Now compare 2026 AI sentiment.

Pew finds:

- **71%** of Americans expect AI to produce **fewer jobs** over the next 20 years.
- only **5%** expect more jobs.
- 52% say they're more concerned than excited about AI.
- only 9% are more excited than concerned. citeturn1search5

That's a profound difference.

### Society currently perceives AI as much more directly threatening to labour than it perceived computers/Internet.

And there is a reason.

The Internet mainly changed **where/how work happened**.

AI can potentially perform portions of the work.

# 5. Then the Web appeared

Tim Berners-Lee's original WorldWideWeb browser in 1990 was actually both a **browser and editor**.

In other words, creation was part of the original conception of the Web.

CERN didn't fund GUI browsers for all the major platforms, so the Web team distributed code and effectively asked volunteers to build them.

Eight volunteers responded with versions for Unix, Mac and PC. citeturn1search3

Already we can see something that keeps repeating:

> **Technology builds tools that make the next generation of technology easier to build.**

# 10. And current adoption is extraordinarily fast

Stanford's 2026 AI Index estimates generative AI reached approximately **53% population-level adoption within three years**, faster than either PCs or the Internet at comparable stages.

It reports organizational AI use rising to **88% in 2025**, although actual AI-agent deployment across business functions remains in the single digits.

That distinction is extremely important. citeturn0search0turn0search12

**late in AI awareness**

**very early in agentic organisational redesign.**

# 11. This looks remarkably similar to Internet adoption

Look at the Internet progression.

In 1995:

> Interesting, growing quickly, but not essential.

By 1998–2000:

> Everyone knows this matters.

By 2000:

> Massive investment, enormous expectations.

Then:

> Dot-com crash.

Many concluded:

> The Internet was overhyped.

But the correct conclusion was:

> **Internet companies were overvalued; the Internet itself was underestimated.**

Brookings made essentially this distinction after the dot-com collapse: failed dot-com companies did not imply that the Internet's underlying economic impact had disappeared; much of its eventual effect would occur through adoption by the existing economy. citeturn1search2

That's an important warning for AI.

# 12. AI can simultaneously be a bubble AND transformative

These aren't opposites.

People often argue:

### Camp A

> “AI is a bubble.”

### Camp B

> “AI will transform everything.”

Both can be correct.

1999 demonstrated exactly that possibility.

Hundreds of Internet companies were ridiculous investments.

The Internet was still revolutionary.

Interestingly, the Federal Reserve recently compared the two periods and noted an important difference: many dot-com companies had little or no realised earnings, whereas today's major AI-associated companies generally already have substantial businesses and earnings.

The Fed nevertheless notes similarities in rapid asset-price appreciation around transformative technology. citeturn1search4

So:

**AI investment boom ≠ proof AI succeeds everywhere.**

But:

**AI investment bubble ≠ proof AI is unimportant.**

# 15. There is another major historical parallel: productivity lag

When computers first spread through companies, economists encountered what became known as the productivity paradox.

Businesses were spending enormous amounts on IT.

Yet aggregate productivity improvements initially looked disappointing.

Eventually economists increasingly recognised that technology alone wasn't enough.

Organisations had to change:

- processes
- workflows
- organisational structures
- skills
- supply chains
- management

Brookings was already discussing this around 2000: decades of cheaper and more powerful computers had not immediately generated obvious productivity improvements; eventually the organisational changes accompanying IT began appearing in productivity statistics. citeturn1search0turn1search2

This may be one of the **most important lessons for AI**.

# 17. Now the biggest difference between Internet and AI

I would summarise it like this:

| Revolution | Scarcity reduced |
|---|---|
| Printing press | information reproduction |
| Industrial Revolution | physical power |
| Electricity | distributed mechanical power |
| Computer | calculation |

# 18. But here I want to challenge one common AI assumption

People say:

> “If everyone can build software, software becomes worthless.”

I don't think that's quite right.

Think about the Internet.

When everyone gained the ability to publish information:

**information did not become worthless.**

Instead:

> publishing became cheap, therefore **attention became scarce**.

That produced Google, SEO, recommendation engines, social networks and enormous advertising markets.

AI may create a similar scarcity inversion.


That is a very different founder problem.

AI improves your strongest capability — building — while potentially making that capability less scarce economically.

# 10. What the simulation therefore must contain

A simple model containing only:

```text
AI ↑
→ productivity ↑
→ workers ↓
```

will produce nonsense.

We need at minimum:

\[
GDP = C + I + G + NX
\]

and household consumption needs something like:

\[
C_t=f(W_t + B_t + K_t - T_t,\; confidence,\; prices)
\]

where:

- **W** = wages/labour income
- **B** = government transfers
- **K** = capital/dividend income
- **T** = tax.

Businesses:

\[
Profit_t = Revenue_t-Wages_t-AIcost_t-OtherCosts_t
\]

Government:

\[
Balance_t = Taxes_t - Transfers_t-G_t
\]

AI productivity:

\[
Output/Worker \uparrow
\]

but then crucially:

\[
Price \downarrow
\rightarrow Demand \uparrow
\rightarrow Output \uparrow
\rightarrow Employment\ response
\]

And we need another term that most AI-job forecasts omit:

# New demand.

Something like:

\[
NewSector_t =
f(CostReduction,\ Technology,\ Capital,\ HumanNeeds,\ Time)
\]

Without that variable, the model would have predicted catastrophic permanent unemployment from mechanisation, computers and the Internet.

## The most important conclusion before simulation

The verified historical data **do not support** a simple law:

> recession → technology → recovery.

They support something more useful:

> **A shock destroys income and demand. The depth of the recession depends partly on how strongly that income-demand feedback is allowed to run. Policy interrupts that loop. Meanwhile, recessions accelerate reallocation away from already-vulnerable sectors. Technologies and business models that were incubating before the crisis can then capture disproportionate investment, labour and demand during the subsequent expansion.**

And now we have something we did **not** have at the beginning of today's discussion:

### a falsifiable AI hypothesis.

If AI removes labour income faster than **(a)** prices fall, **(b)** new demand forms, **(c)** new sectors absorb labour, **(d)** capital income reaches households, or **(e)** government recycles the surplus, then:

**wages ↓ → consumption ↓ → revenue ↓ → investment ↓ → employment ↓ → tax ↓ → recessionary feedback.**

If productivity gains circulate sufficiently through those five channels, we instead get:

**productivity ↑ → prices ↓ / real income ↑ → consumption/new demand ↑ → investment ↑ → new economic activity ↑ → employment restructures rather than disappears.**

That is the **historical fork** our US–UK–India AI simulation should test. The important input will not merely be “AI automates X%.” It will be **how fast automation happens versus how fast purchasing power and new demand are regenerated.**

# 2. Your 1929 correction is important

The Depression cannot be reduced to:

> stock market crashed → recession.

The official Federal Reserve history itself gives a much richer sequence.

During the 1920s, automobiles, telephones and other new technologies were spreading rapidly. Brokerage houses, investment trusts and margin accounts expanded access to equities. Investors commonly put down only around **10%** and borrowed the remainder. citeturn0search1

Meanwhile, the Fed had loosened conditions in 1924 and 1927 partly to support recovery and the post-WWI international monetary system. By 1928 it became worried about financial speculation and tightened. citeturn0search4turn0search5

Then:

**WWI aftermath / gold-standard problems → credit conditions → 1920s productivity & technology boom → equity speculation/leverage → monetary tightening → 1929 contraction/crash → consumption/investment decline → banking panics → money supply collapse → deflation → real debt burden ↑ → bankruptcies → unemployment → demand ↓↓**

The banking panics are critical. The Fed's history says the economy appeared capable of recovering from the initial crash before banking crises beginning in late 1930 transformed the downturn into the Great Depression. citeturn0search7

And from fall 1930 to winter 1933:

**money supply fell nearly 30%.** citeturn0search0

So this is already teaching our simulation something:

> **A shock alone doesn't determine the outcome. Feedback loops and policy response determine amplification.**

> “$650bn/year is flowing into AI infrastructure. What economic outcomes are necessary to justify that capital?”

Then model:

\[
Investment
\rightarrow RequiredReturn
\rightarrow RequiredRevenue
\rightarrow RequiredAIUsage
\rightarrow RequiredProductivity
\]

Reverse the equation.

That's interesting.

Instead of predicting AI from hype, ask:

> **What must become true for today's valuation/investment to make economic sense?**

Then compare required outcomes against historical technology transitions.

That could expose both:

**underpriced opportunities** and **unrealistic expectations.**

# What should happen next

This time I would deliberately **not code**.

### Phase 0 — Research Charter

Freeze:

**question  
hypotheses  
model boundaries  
definitions  
evidence hierarchy  
validation criteria  
failure criteria.**

### Phase 1 — Data Catalogue

For every variable:

**source / API or download / country / start date / frequency / units / revisions / licence / confidence.**

### Phase 2 — Historical Master Panel

Build:

**US 1929–2026  
UK 1948–2026  
India 1950/51–2026**

plus historical-extension dataset before those dates.

### Phase 3 — Descriptive transition maps

No prediction yet.

Make the data explain:

**where output went  
where workers went  
where capital went  
where wages went  
where profits went.**

### Phase 4 — Causal hypotheses

Test the relationships we've been discussing.

### Phase 5 — History Replay

1980 → 2008 → 2020.

Include hidden labour.

### Phase 6 — Blind historical forecasting

Pretend we're in 1995.

Pretend we're in 2006.

Pretend we're in 2019.

See what the model actually knows.

### Phase 7 — AI transition scenarios

Only now feed AI into it.

### Phase 8 — Commercial experiment

**Before building a polished SaaS**, turn one result into a small usable decision tool and test whether anybody values the answer.

That last line matters particularly for us.

# My current position on the money-run question

**Don't sell the dataset. Don't sell “AI prediction.” Don't sell another dashboard.**

The potentially valuable asset is:

> **A historically calibrated transition engine that shows how a technological shock could propagate through capital, companies, labour and households—and what decisions remain robust under uncertainty.**

That is sufficiently different from the generic:

> “AI will replace 37% of jobs”

conversation.

And it uses almost everything we have discovered on this page instead of throwing the work away.

The historical research becomes the **evidence layer**.

History Replay becomes the **trust layer**.

The economic model becomes the **reasoning layer**.

Scenario simulation becomes the **product layer**.

Decision guidance becomes the **human-value layer**.

And the one thing I would explicitly **not** promise is prediction certainty. The product's value would be helping someone make a better decision **despite uncertainty**, rather than pretending uncertainty has disappeared.

That, I think, is the first coherent destination this page has reached.
